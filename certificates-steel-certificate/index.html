Manufacturing technology

How steel is manufactured can have an impact on its structural performance and can be useful to know when assessing an existing steel element. The two most common manufacturing methods for steel and their nuances are given below.

• Hot-rolled steel

Hot-rolled steel is produced by heating steel billets above their recrystallization temperature and rolling them into shapes such as I-beams, channels, and plates. This high-temperature process refines the grain structure and results in a ductile, tough material suitable for primary load-bearing members. However, uneven cooling during manufacture can introduce residual stresses that influence local buckling and distortion, especially after welding or cutting. Knowing how a section was hot-rolled helps engineers anticipate these internal stresses, assess dimensional tolerances, and design appropriate connections.

• Cold-formed steel

Cold-formed steel is made by bending thin steel sheets at room temperature into shapes such as C- and Z-sections used in light-gauge framing and secondary members. The cold forming process increases the material’s strength through strain hardening but also introduces residual stresses and thin-walled geometries that make the sections prone to local and distortional buckling. Because of this, engineers must understand how the material was formed to correctly predict load capacity and stability. Cold forming can also affect weldability and corrosion protection, particularly at bent edges where the coating or galvanization may be stretched or damaged. Awareness of the manufacturing process allows for proper detailing, welding procedures, and corrosion control to maintain long-term reliability.

Mill certificate

The mill certificate contains useful information which can be crucial to assessing the steel in the future, some of the key properties included on a mill certificate are listed below with the reasons for their inclusion.

• Certification Body and Standards

Certification bodies and standards are organizations and frameworks, such as ISO, ASTM, or EN, that verify the quality, composition, and performance of manufactured steel. Knowing which body certified a material ensures it meets the required strength, ductility, and chemical standards for structural use. This knowledge provides traceability and confidence that the steel has been tested and produced under controlled conditions, reducing the risk of defects or inconsistencies.

• Elongation

Elongation measures the ductility of steel, expressed as the percentage increase in length when a test piece is pulled to failure. Because the specimen is tested to destruction, it gives a true measure of how much the steel can stretch before breaking. This confirms that the steel meets the required specification for ductility, ensuring it can deform safely under load without sudden failure.

• Tensile Strength

Tensile strength, or ultimate tensile strength (UTS), is the maximum stress the steel can withstand before fracture. As this value comes from a test piece pulled to destruction, it provides the true strength of the material. It confirms that the steel meets the required specification for strength and can safely resist the maximum design loads without failure.

• Yield Point

The yield point is the stress level where the steel begins to deform permanently, marking the transition from elastic to plastic behaviour. Determined through destructive testing, it represents the true onset of yielding. This confirms that the steel meets the specified yield strength, ensuring it performs as expected under design loads and only deforms under excessive stress.

• Carbon (Alloy content)

High carbon content increases strength and hardness by forming more cementite (Fe₃C) in the steel microstructure, which resists deformation. However, this comes at the cost of ductility, making the steel less able to deform without cracking under load. Weldability also decreases with higher carbon, because the heat from welding can lead to hard, brittle microstructures in the heat-affected zone (HAZ), increasing the risk of cracking. As a result, steels with high carbon content can become brittle under dynamic or impact loading, or during processes like bending, shaping, or welding.

• Copper (Alloy content)

Copper enhances corrosion resistance, particularly in atmospheric conditions, by forming a protective oxide layer on the steel surface. However, when present in high concentrations, copper can cause hot shortness, a condition where the steel becomes prone to cracking during hot working (like forging or rolling) due to copper segregating at grain boundaries and melting locally. This compromises ductility during high-temperature processing, and can lead to brittleness in regions affected by thermal cycling or fabrication. In terms of weldability, copper isn't usually a major problem unless present in excess, but it can contribute to cracking if it segregates in the weld zone.

• Magnesium (Alloy content)

Magnesium is generally present only in very small trace amounts in steel, mainly for deoxidation removing unwanted oxygen during steelmaking. But if magnesium content is too high, it can form hard, brittle inclusions such as magnesium oxides. These inclusions act as stress concentrators, reducing both ductility and fatigue resistance, and potentially leading to brittle failure under cyclic loading. They may also interfere with welding quality, as inclusions can create weak spots or porosity in welds, although this is less common due to magnesium’s limited use.

• Phosphorus (Alloy content)

Phosphorus increases strength and machinability, and in small amounts can help with corrosion resistance in low-alloy steels. However, it drastically reduces ductility and toughness, especially at low temperatures, due to its tendency to segregate at grain boundaries and create weak, brittle zones. This leads to a much higher risk of brittle fracture, particularly in cold environments or under sudden impact loads. Weldability also suffers, since phosphorus can make the weld metal and heat-affected zone more prone to cracking and reduce the integrity of welded joints.

• Silicon (Alloy content)

Silicon improves strength and elasticity (especially in spring steels), by increasing solid-solution strengthening and supporting the formation of ferrite.
