Aggregates and admixtures can have long term effects on the performance and durability of a structure. It can be essential to know what components have been used in concrete to accurately assess a structure. Information to be included about the admixture and aggregate types could include, but is not limited to:

• Blast Furnace Slag (Admixture)

Recording the presence of blast furnace slag (GGBS) is crucial, as it directly influences the concrete’s early-age strength development and long-term durability. High replacement levels can significantly delay strength gain, which may be critical during early construction phases or formwork removal. However, slag-rich concrete tends to have lower permeability and better chemical resistance, making it highly durable in sulphate-rich environments, where standard Portland cement may degrade. The reduced permeability helps protect reinforcement from corrosion, especially in aggressive soils or groundwater. That said, improper curing or high slag content can increase the risk of autogenous shrinkage, potentially leading to microcracking, which may offset some of the corrosion resistance by allowing moisture and sulphates to penetrate locally.

• Burnt Shale (Admixture)

Inclusion of burnt shale as a cementitious component can introduce variability in strength, porosity, and water absorption. These factors affect the concrete’s resistance to freeze-thaw damage and chemical attack, particularly in environments with sulphates or chlorides. If porosity increases, it can allow moisture and oxygen to penetrate, raising the risk of steel reinforcement corrosion. Variable quality may also cause inconsistent mechanical properties, which could lead to uneven cracking or localized brittle zones.

• Clinker (Cement Constituent)

High clinker content increases the heat of hydration, which can lead to thermal cracking, especially in large pours or hot weather. Shrinkage cracking from high clinker levels creates direct pathways for moisture and aggressive agents to reach embedded steel, increasing the likelihood of reinforcement corrosion. While it may boost early strength, excessive clinker without supplementary materials can reduce long-term durability, particularly in harsh environmental exposures.

• Granite (Aggregate)

Granite is a very hard and dense natural aggregate that provides high compressive strength and stiffness, improving the long-term performance of structural concrete. Its low porosity helps reduce permeability and water absorption, enhancing durability against freeze–thaw and chemical attack. However, granite’s relatively high thermal expansion coefficient can increase the risk of thermal cracking in large elements if not properly detailed. When used with cement containing alkalis, some granite types can contribute to alkali–silica reaction (ASR), so aggregate reactivity testing is essential to avoid long-term expansion and cracking.

• Limestone (Aggregate)

Limestone is often used in small amounts as a filler or minor cement component, and in many cases it is benign. However, if used in high volumes without appropriate supplementary materials, it can reduce strength, especially in aggressive environments. Lower strength and potential increased permeability can allow ingress of carbon dioxide or chlorides, raising the risk of corrosion in embedded steel. It may also reduce resistance to chemical attack if the mix isn’t properly designed for the exposure class.

• Natural Calcined Pozzolana (Admixture)

The reactivity of calcined pozzolana can vary widely depending on its mineralogical source and processing quality. If poorly reactive, it may fail to densify the microstructure, leading to higher permeability and increased risk of corrosion. Inconsistent performance can also affect long-term strength and shrinkage behaviour, possibly resulting in microcracking and reduced durability in chloride-laden or moist environments.

• Natural Pozzolana (Admixture)

Like its calcined counterpart, natural pozzolana can have highly variable properties. If not properly tested and controlled, it can lead to inconsistent strength gain and reduce the concrete’s ability to resist chemical attack or chloride ingress. This can compromise durability, especially near the coast or in de-icing environments. Increased porosity from poor-quality pozzolana may also heighten the risk of reinforcement corrosion, especially in thin or lightly covered sections.

• Pulverised Fuel Ash (Admixture)

Pulverised Fuel Ash (PFA) improves workability, long-term strength, and resistance to chloride ingress, making it highly beneficial for corrosion protection of steel reinforcement. However, excessive amounts may significantly delay early strength, affecting formwork removal or construction scheduling. It may also increase the risk of early-age shrinkage cracking if not properly cured, potentially creating paths for moisture and oxygen to reach reinforcement and trigger corrosion over time.

• Recycled Concrete Aggregate (Aggregate)

Recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) contains old cement paste attached to original aggregate, resulting in higher porosity and water absorption. Over time, this can increase shrinkage, creep, and permeability, accelerating carbonation and corrosion risks in reinforcement. While RCA contributes to sustainability, its use in structural concrete requires strict control of quality and moisture condition. Long-term performance depends heavily on source material quality and mix design adjustments to offset reduced density and strength.

• Silica Fume (Admixture)

Silica fume greatly enhances strength, impermeability, and chemical resistance due to its ultra-fine particle size and high pozzolanic activity. However, it also increases brittleness and the risk of autogenous and drying shrinkage, especially if water curing is insufficient. These fine microcracks can compromise the durability of high-performance concrete by providing localized routes for moisture or chlorides to reach the steel, potentially initiating corrosion, especially in highly stressed or thin concrete sections.

As well as the presence and percentage of these materials its source and manufacturer should be included when possible.
