Aluminium certificate

Page 54 of 56 - Certificates

Example certificate, this information should be stored in an XML, or CSV format

Aluminium alloys are widely used in engineering applications due to their excellent strength-to-weight ratio, corrosion resistance, and versatility in forming and joining processes. The mechanical and physical properties of aluminium can be significantly modified by alloying with various elements, each contributing distinct characteristics that influence performance under different conditions. Understanding the role of these alloying elements is crucial to determine factors such as strength, ductility, weldability, and fatigue resistance. This section outlines the effects of typical alloying elements on aluminium’s behaviour, particularly in relation to structural integrity, fabrication processes, and long-term durability. Aluminium should be treated similarly to steel regarding the retainment of information, there are some differences in alloys and their effect between steel and aluminium, the common alloys of aluminium are given below.

• Copper

Copper increases strength and hardness, especially after heat treatment (common in 2xxx series aluminium alloys). However, it reduces ductility, making the material less able to deform without cracking. It also decreases weldability, as copper-rich regions can lead to hot cracking during welding. This combination means alloys with high copper content are more brittle under impact or fatigue loads, and they’re not ideal for welded structural connections.

• Magnesium

Magnesium is one of the most effective elements for strengthening aluminium while still maintaining good ductility. It also improves weldability, especially compared to copper or zinc. In moderate amounts, it does not significantly increase brittleness, which is why aluminium-magnesium alloys (5xxx series) are often used in structural applications where forming and welding are required. However, excessive magnesium can lead to strain hardening and reduce fatigue resistance over time.

• Manganese

Manganese slightly increases strength and corrosion resistance without heavily compromising ductility or weldability. It helps prevent brittleness by stabilizing grain structure and reducing the formation of harmful intermetallic compounds. Because of this balance, manganese is often used in structural aluminium alloys where a balance of toughness and formability is needed (e.g., 3xxx series).

• Silicon

Silicon lowers melting temperature and improves castability, making it ideal for casting alloys (like 4xx.x series). When used in moderation, it doesn’t severely impact ductility or weldability. However, in high concentrations, silicon can form brittle phases, reducing ductility and increasing brittleness, especially under tension or impact. Weldability can also suffer if these brittle phases concentrate at grain boundaries.

• Zinc

Zinc significantly increases strength, especially when combined with magnesium (forming high-strength 7xxx series alloys). But this comes at the cost of ductility, especially if over-aged or exposed to corrosive environments. Weldability is poor, because zinc can cause hot cracking and porosity during welding. These alloys also tend to be more brittle, especially under stress-corrosion or fatigue loading, which limits their use in some structural applications without additional treatment or protection.
